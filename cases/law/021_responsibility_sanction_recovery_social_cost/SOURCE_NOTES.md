# LAW-010 Source Notes — Responsibility, Sanction, Recovery, Emotion, Social Cost, and Death-Penalty Status

Status: source packet completed for first-pass universal analysis.

## 1. ICC — punishment and reparations are distinct routes

Sources:

- Rome Statute Article 75 — reparations to victims, including restitution, compensation and rehabilitation;
- Rome Statute Article 77 — applicable penalties for a convicted person;
- ICC victim-representation manual and reparations-case materials.

Use in LAW-010:

`penalty != victim reparation`.

The same conviction can support both routes without collapsing them into one legal object.

URLs:
- https://www.icc-cpi.int/sites/default/files/2024-05/Rome-Statute-eng.pdf

## 2. UN human-rights redress — reparation is plural

Source family: OHCHR / treaty-body remedial materials, including CAT General Comment No. 3 and Human Rights Committee remedy/reparation guidance.

Relevant source-side structure:

- restitution;
- compensation;
- rehabilitation;
- satisfaction;
- guarantees of non-repetition;
- victim status is not universally conditioned on the perpetrator being identified, apprehended, prosecuted or convicted.

Use in LAW-010:

`reparation != money only`;

`punishment/prosecution != complete victim repair`;

`responsibility established != universal prerequisite for victim status/redress`.

URLs:
- https://www.ohchr.org/en/documents/general-comments-and-recommendations/catcgc3-general-comment-no-3-2012-implementation-article-14
- https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/basic-principles-and-guidelines-right-remedy-and-reparation

## 3. South Africa — S v Makwanyane

Source: Constitutional Court of South Africa, `S v Makwanyane and Another`, 6 June 1995.

Relevant source-side structure:

- the Court was prepared to assume that a majority of South Africans supported capital punishment in extreme murder cases;
- public opinion could have relevance but was not a substitute for constitutional adjudication;
- the Court considered the irredeemable character of death, the possibility of error/arbitrariness, inequality in practical enforcement, dignity, and the right to life;
- the death penalty was held unconstitutional.

Use in LAW-010:

`strong public support / condemnation pressure != constitutionally decisive result`.

The source supports modeling public emotion/attitude as a legally or politically relevant input without treating it as the judgment rule itself.

URL: https://www.saflii.org/za/cases/ZACC/1995/3.html

## 4. Germany — Article 102 and Nazi-era abuse

Sources:

- Basic Law, Article 102: `Capital punishment is abolished.`
- German Bundestag historical record on 1952 attempts to reintroduce capital punishment.
- Federal Constitutional Court judgment of 21 June 1977 discussing the historical reason for Article 102.

Relevant source-side structure:

- the Bundestag records that the framers of the Basic Law acted under the impression of the many death sentences during Nazi rule;
- the Federal Constitutional Court later stated that abuse of capital punishment under the Nazi regime was decisive for inserting Article 102;
- postwar political debate nevertheless contained retributive, deterrent and public-demand arguments for reintroduction.

Use in LAW-010:

`historical experience of coercive abuse` and `contemporary retributive/public pressure` can coexist and push legal policy in opposite directions.

This is a strong witness for preserving historical state-abuse memory separately from present public sentiment.

URLs:

- https://www.bundestag.de/dokumente/textarchiv/1952-10-02-todesstrafe-209552

## 5. Comparative status — abolition in law vs practice

Source: Amnesty International comparative death-penalty reporting.

As of the end of 2025, Amnesty reported 113 countries abolitionist for all crimes and 145 countries abolitionist in law or practice.

The source family distinguishes:

- abolitionist for all crimes;
- abolitionist for ordinary crimes only;
- abolitionist in practice;
- retentionist.

Use in LAW-010:

This defeats a single binary representation:

`death penalty exists in statute <-> executions occur`.

A jurisdiction can formally retain a death penalty while functioning as abolitionist in practice for a sustained period.

URLs:
- https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/05/death-penalty-in-2025-facts-and-figures/
- https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/act50/9240/2025/en/

## 6. Victim-family heterogeneity

Source: US Office of Justice Programs virtual-library record for `Dignity Denied` and related victim-source materials.

Relevant source-side structure:

- murder-victim family members who oppose capital punishment form a real subgroup;
- their interests can conflict with a prosecution seeking the death penalty;
- victim status therefore does not imply one sentencing preference.

Use in LAW-010:

`victim-family status + grief != one mandatory punishment preference`.

Emotion remains actor-indexed and must not be normalized into one aggregate victim value without a separate empirical/normative rule.

URL:
- https://ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/dignity-denied-experience-murder-victims-family-members-who-oppose

## 7. Source discipline

These sources are not used to prove one global theory of punishment.

They establish separate witness patterns:

1. punishment and victim reparation can be legally distinct;
2. reparation itself can have several forms;
3. victim status/redress need not wait for perpetrator conviction in every regime;
4. strong public sentiment can support retention without controlling constitutional adjudication;
5. historical abuse of state killing can support abolition or constitutional distrust while present retributive pressure persists;
6. law-on-the-books and actual execution practice can diverge;
7. victim-family emotion is heterogeneous rather than one legal input value.

Full generalization remains open to counterexamples.
