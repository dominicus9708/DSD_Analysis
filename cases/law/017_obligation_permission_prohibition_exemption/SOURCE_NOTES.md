# LAW-006 Source Notes — Obligation, Permission, Prohibition, and Exemption

## 1. Source discipline

This case does not begin from one jurisdiction's statute. It tests a universal structural candidate against several independent international/transnational witness families and a formal deontic-logic counterpressure source.

The source-side vocabulary remains primary. DSD terminology is added only after the source distinctions are fixed.

## 2. ILO Forced Labour Convention No. 29

Official source: ILO NORMLEX, Convention C029.

Relevant structure:

- Article 1: ratifying members undertake to suppress forced or compulsory labour.
- Article 2(1): defines forced or compulsory labour.
- Article 2(2): excludes specified categories from that definition, including purely military compulsory service, normal civic obligations, specified court-conviction labour under conditions, emergency work, and minor communal services under conditions.
- Article 25: illegal exaction of forced or compulsory labour is to be punishable, and ratifying members have an obligation to ensure adequate and strictly enforced penalties.

Source-side result:

`work can be compulsory in fact`

`!= work falls inside the Convention's prohibited category`

because the source rule contains scope exclusions/conditions.

The exception is not accurately represented by saying that the same act is first globally `PROHIBITED` and then globally `PERMITTED`. The treaty first defines the scope of the regulated category and then supplies consequences.

## 3. ICCPR / Human Rights Committee

Official source family: OHCHR treaty materials and Human Rights Committee General Comments.

Relevant structures:

### Rights and restrictions

ICCPR Article 19 recognizes freedom of expression while allowing only specified restrictions meeting legal and necessity conditions. Human Rights Committee General Comment No. 34 develops this distinction.

Source-side result:

`a protected right/liberty exists`

`!= every exercise is unrestricted in every context`.

A right can coexist with rule-conditioned restrictions without the underlying right being reduced to one unconditional permission bit.

### Derogation and non-derogability

ICCPR Article 4 and Human Rights Committee General Comment No. 29 allow temporary derogation from some Covenant obligations only under strict conditions in a public emergency, while other obligations/rights remain non-derogable and derogation itself is subject to safeguards.

Source-side result:

`ordinary obligation/status`

`!= emergency derogation authority`

`!= unrestricted suspension`.

A derogation route is an indexed exception power with conditions, not a global negation of the original norm.

## 4. UNIDROIT Principles — public permission

Official source: UNIDROIT Principles, Chapter 6 Section 1, Articles 6.1.14-6.1.17.

Relevant structure:

- Article 6.1.14: where State law requires public permission affecting validity or performance, a specified party must take necessary measures to obtain it.
- Article 6.1.15: that party must act without undue delay and may have a notice duty.
- Article 6.1.16: where permission is neither granted nor refused, either party may have a termination entitlement under stated conditions.
- Article 6.1.17: refusal has different consequences depending on whether permission affects validity or only performance.

This source is particularly useful because it places **permission** and **obligation** in the same structure without identifying them.

The party may be:

`obligated to seek permission`

while the underlying performance is:

`not yet legally permitted/effective under the public-permission regime`.

Thus:

`obligation to seek P`

`!= P already granted`

and

`P not yet granted`

`!= P refused`.

Official source:
https://www.unidroit.org/instruments/commercial-contracts/unidroit-principles-2010/chapter-6-section-1/

## 5. Formal deontic-logic counterpressure

Source family: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Deontic Logic and Modal Logic entries.

Relevant points:

- deontic logic treats notions such as obligation, permission, prohibition, optionality, non-obligation, claims, liberties, powers, immunities, and responsibility;
- standard systems often define prohibition/permission through obligation and commonly accept a consistency principle such as `O(A) -> P(A)`;
- stronger theories distinguish strong permission from mere absence of prohibition;
- the field contains well-known problems involving conflicts, contrary-to-duty obligations, normative gaps, free-choice permission, and agency.

This is used as **counterpressure**, not as positive law.

It defeats a naive model in which `OBLIGATORY`, `PERMITTED`, `PROHIBITED`, and `EXEMPT` are always four disjoint legal values.

Sources:
https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2021/entries/logic-deontic/
https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2006/entries/logic-modal/

## 6. Source-side synthesis

The selected witnesses support the following weak cross-system candidate:

1. causal/physical possibility is not legal permission;
2. legal permission is not legal obligation;
3. absence of an obligation is not automatically a positive permission unless the source logic/rule says so;
4. prohibition depends on a rule's applicability and scope;
5. exception/exemption/derogation can modify applicability, duty, or consequence without becoming one universal opposite status;
6. the same action description can have different normative statuses for different actors, purposes, times, conditions, and rule sources.

They do **not** establish one universal algebra of deontic operators.
