## 1. Primary source

David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, Book I, Part I, Section I.

Primary text consulted via Project Gutenberg.

Relevant source structure:

- Hume treats simple impressions as prior to corresponding simple ideas as a general principle.
- He introduces one 'contradictory phenomenon': a person has experienced all shades of blue except one.
- The experienced shades are arranged in gradual order, leaving a perceptible gap.
- Hume judges that imagination can supply the missing shade and treats this as a singular exception to the general maxim.

The primary source itself therefore supplies both the missing direct corresponding impression and a highly structured neighboring color series.

## 2. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Hume

The archived 2012 Hume entry gives an explicit **mental-mixing** interpretation:

- neighboring shade ideas may be mentally mixed analogously to physical color mixing;
- the result can remain a simple idea even though its formation uses two already available simple ideas;
- the missing shade has no direct antecedent impression but is not independent of impressions.

This is a close external match to the sealed DSD distinction:

`simple content != non-derived formation history`.

The Summer 2026 SEP entry emphasizes that the example is unusual because it involves **gradation** and need not generalize to the broader uses of the Copy Principle.

## 3. Robert J. Fogelin — color-space / gap structure

Robert J. Fogelin, 'Hume and the Missing Shade of Blue', discussed in *Philosophical Interpretations*.

The Oxford Academic abstract states that simple color ideas may resemble one another in degrees without losing simplicity, and that colors therefore form a space in which gaps can be recognized and, when large enough, filled.

This directly supports the sealed prediction that the ordered relational neighborhood is substantive formation-supporting structure.

## 4. Hume Texts Online scholarship survey

The Hume Texts Online survey identifies two especially relevant historical routes:

- Robert Cummins interprets having the idea in terms of **recognitional capacity**;
- Don Garrett's alternative is based on **natural resemblances among simple ideas**.

This is important for the DSD distinction between:

- locating/recognizing the missing item;
- possessing the exact qualitative simple idea;
- explaining the formation route.

The survey also shows that the debate is not exhausted by a single interpretation.

## 5. Emily Kelahan — theoretical-posits interpretation

Emily Kelahan, 'Simple Ideas and Hume's Missing Shade of Blue', *Philosophia* 44 (2016), 809–825.

The abstract argues that Humean simple ideas are fruitfully understood as **theoretical posits**, and that this interpretation dissolves the apparent threat of the missing shade to the Copy Principle.

This is not identical to the DSD formation-history distinction, but it confirms that the status and meaning of 'simple idea' is itself a live interpretive variable.

## 6. Jonathan Finch — empirical test

Jonathan Finch, 'A Test: Hume's Missing Shade of Blue', *Journal of Scottish Philosophy* 13 (2015), 219–228.

The abstract reports an empirical test in which 72 students attempted to determine missing shade ratios under controlled conditions. Performance varied, with only a small subset succeeding on the most difficult condition.

This shows that the purported ability is not merely a logical transition. Its actual reliability can be treated as an empirical/psychological question.

## 7. Masato Narita — resemblance thesis vs causal thesis

Masato Narita, 'Hume's First Principle and the Missing Shade of Blue' (2015, Japanese journal article; English abstract available through J-STAGE).

Narita explicitly separates a **resemblance thesis** and a **causal thesis** within Hume's first principle, and argues that the missing shade is not a counterexample to the former but is a counterexample to the latter.

This distinction was **not explicitly predicted in the BENCH-C02 seal** and is retained as a C3-level miss/refinement.

## 8. Brian D. Earp — methodological survival of the Copy Principle

Brian D. Earp, 'Hume's Missing Shade of Blue: A New Solution', *Journal of Scottish Philosophy* 18 (2020), 91–104.

The abstract argues that even if the missing shade is a true counterexample, Hume need not revise the entire theory because of the methodological resources and empiricist aims of the larger project.

This supports the sealed prediction that DSD should not simply declare the example invalid or treat one exceptional formation route as automatically destroying the broader empirical generalization.
