# Platform Procedural Governance — Source Addendum

Status: candidate source addendum; metadata and exact page support must be frozen before publication.

Purpose: support the actor-neutral platform-governance extension without inferring malicious intent from profit motive or singling out a named platform in the core argument.

## Governance and legitimacy

1. Kate Klonick, “The New Governors: The People, Rules, and Processes Governing Online Speech,” *Harvard Law Review* 131 (2018).
   - Use: platforms as private governance structures for online speech.
   - Control: governance analogy does not make a platform legally identical to a state.

2. Nicolas Suzor, Tess Van Geelen, and Sarah Myers West, “Evaluating the Legitimacy of Platform Governance: A Review of Research and a Shared Research Agenda,” *International Communication Gazette* (2018).
   - DOI: 10.1177/1748048518757142
   - Use: legitimacy criteria for online intermediary governance.

## Procedural fairness, notice, reasons, appeal, and review

3. Judit Bayer, “Procedural rights as safeguard for human rights in platform regulation,” *Policy & Internet* (2022).
   - DOI: 10.1002/poi3.298
   - Use: notice, reasons, complaint, challenge, transparency, and procedural safeguards in platform regulation.

4. Rory Van Loo, “Federal Rules of Platform Procedure” (2020).
   - Use: platforms as private dispute-resolution systems; procedural minimums such as notice, reasonable investigation, accountability, and review.

5. Catalina Goanta and Pietro Ortolani, “Unpacking Content Moderation: the Rise of Social Media Platforms as Online Civil Courts.”
   - DOI: 10.2139/ssrn.3969360
   - Use: content moderation as private dispute resolution; transparency and legitimacy problems.

## Sanction trajectories and recurrence

6. Jonathan P. Chang and Cristian Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil, “Trajectories of Blocked Community Members: Redemption, Recidivism and Departure,” *The Web Conference* (2019).
   - DOI: 10.1145/3308558.3313638
   - Use: blocked users can reform, recidivate, or leave; sanction outcomes are not one state.

7. Shagun Jhaver, Sucheta Ghoshal, Amy Bruckman, and Eric Gilbert, “Online Harassment and Content Moderation: The Case of Blocklists,” *ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction* 25 (2018).
   - DOI: 10.1145/3185593
   - Use: strengths and weaknesses of user blocking/blocklists; protection and overblocking trade-offs.

## Incentive and engagement conflict

8. George Beknazar-Yuzbashev, Rafael Jiménez-Durán, and Mateusz Stalinski, “A Model of Harmful Yet Engaging Content on Social Media,” *AEA Papers and Proceedings* 114 (2024).
   - DOI: 10.1257/pandp.20241004
   - Use: under some advertising-driven structures, harmful content can be privately profitable when complementary to time spent; `engagement != welfare`.

9. Luke Munn, “Angry by design: toxic communication and technical architectures,” *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* (2020).
   - DOI: 10.1057/s41599-020-00550-7
   - Use: platform architecture and engagement design as structural contributors to antagonistic communication.

## DSD claim controls

These sources support external comparison only.

Do not infer:
- `large company -> unlimited operational capacity`;
- `profit motive -> malicious intent`;
- `private governance -> state-equivalent legal duty`;
- `report -> guilt`;
- `sanction -> rehabilitation`;
- `removal count -> conflict resolved`.

Preserve instead:

`Resource != OperationalCapacity != Authority != Responsibility`

`Allow != Recommend != Amplify != Monetize != Deprioritize != Remove != Sanction`

`ContentModerated != ConflictResolved`

`non-performance != incapacity`

and require explicit evidence for named-actor claims.
